Why the greatest love poems last

By the A Poem About Love editors · Updated November 17, 2026 · 2 min read

Write a love poem freeGet published
Quick answer

The love poems that last share three things: a specific speaker talking to a specific person, a single image or argument carried all the way through, and an honest admission of fear, of time, loss or change, underneath the praise.

On this page

  2. Shakespeare: argue with time
  3. John Donne: be outrageous
  4. Elizabeth Barrett Browning: count it out

Most love poems are forgotten within a week. A handful have lasted hundreds, even thousands, of years. They weren't simply luckier. They share a few habits any writer can learn from.

Sappho: the body tells the truth

Writing on the island of Lesbos around 600 BCE, Sappho described love the way it actually feels: a tongue that breaks, a thin fire under the skin, ears that ring. Twenty-six centuries later the description still works, because she wrote down symptoms, not ideas.

Borrow this: describe what love does to your body, not what it “is.”

Shakespeare: argue with time

Sonnet 18, “Shall I compare thee to a summer's day?”, is really an argument. Summer is too short, too hot and too changeable, so the poem says the beloved will live on in the poem itself instead.

“So long lives this, and this gives life to thee.”William Shakespeare, Sonnet 18 (public domain)

Borrow this: give your poem a problem to solve, then solve it in the last line.

John Donne: be outrageous

In “The Sun Rising,” Donne scolds the sun for waking him and his lover, calling it a “busy old fool.” It's funny, bold and intimate all at once. Love poems don't have to be solemn.

Borrow this: let yourself be playful, even cheeky.

Elizabeth Barrett Browning: count it out

“How do I love thee? Let me count the ways.” Sonnet 43 is a list, and lists are wonderful for love poems because each item is a small proof. She wrote these sonnets during her courtship with the poet Robert Browning.

Borrow this: make a list of ways, places or reasons, and let it build.

Emily Dickinson: say less

Dickinson's “Wild nights – Wild nights!” is only twelve short lines long, and it burns. She trusted dashes, gaps and silence to do half the work.

Borrow this: cut. Then cut again.

What they all share

  • A real you being spoken to
  • One controlling image or argument
  • Fear underneath the joy, about time, distance or loss
  • Precise, physical words

Shakespeare's Sonnet 18, which begins “Shall I compare thee to a summer's day?”, and Elizabeth Barrett Browning's Sonnet 43, “How Do I Love Thee?”, are among the most famous love poems in English.

Poems by writers like Shakespeare, Donne, Browning and Dickinson are in the public domain in the United States and can be quoted and shared freely.
