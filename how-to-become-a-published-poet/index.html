How to become a published poet, step by step

Eight steps from notebook to print: polishing your poems, finding journals, surviving rejection, and publishing your first book.

See the 8 stepsGet published here first
Quick answer

To become a published poet, finish a set of strong poems, read the journals you admire, send 3 to 5 poems at a time following each journal's guidelines, track your submissions, and keep going through rejection. Then gather your poems into a chapbook or full collection.

3–5poems per submission
15–20finished poems before you start
~15–30pages in a chapbook
50+pages in a full collection

The roadmap

  1. 1

    Build a body of poems you'd stand behind

    Write a lot and revise more. Before you send anything out, aim for 15 to 20 finished poems so you can choose your strongest. Put each one away for a week, then read it aloud and cut whatever doesn't earn its place.

  2. 2

    Read the places you want to be published

    Every journal has a taste. Read a few recent issues, online or in print, before you submit. If you'd be proud to sit next to the poems in it, it's a good fit.

  3. 3

    Prepare a clean submission

    Most journals ask for 3 to 5 poems in one file, each starting on a new page, in a plain readable font. Add a two-line cover letter and a short third-person bio. Follow each journal's guidelines exactly. Editors notice.

  4. 4

    Find open calls

    Use the Poets & Writers literary magazine database, Duotrope or the journals' own websites to find open reading periods. Many journals take submissions through Submittable.

  5. 5

    Submit, track, repeat

    Keep a simple spreadsheet: poem, journal, date sent, response. Many journals accept simultaneous submissions, but if a poem is accepted somewhere, withdraw it everywhere else right away.

  6. 6

    Expect rejection and keep going

    Rejection is the normal path for every poet who has ever published, not a verdict on you. A form rejection usually means “not this time,” so send the poem to the next journal on your list.

  7. 7

    Collect your poems into a chapbook

    A chapbook is a short collection, often around 15 to 30 pages. Many small presses run chapbook contests and open reading periods. It's a common first book for poets.

  8. 8

    Publish a full collection, traditionally or yourself

    Full-length poetry books usually run about 50 to 80 pages or more. You can submit to presses and contests, or self-publish through print-on-demand services such as Amazon KDP or IngramSpark and keep full control.

Traditional publishing or self-publishing?

Neither one is “real” and the other “fake.” They're different trade-offs. Plenty of poets do both.

Traditional

  • +An editor chooses your work, which brings credibility
  • +The press handles design, printing and some distribution
  • +Eligible for many awards and reviews
  • –Slow: months to years
  • –Very competitive, with lots of rejection
  • –Less control over cover, price and timing

Self-publishing

  • +You decide everything, and fast
  • +Higher royalty per copy
  • +Great for gifts, readings and a personal audience
  • –You pay for editing and design if you want them
  • –You do all the marketing
  • –Fewer bookstores and reviewers will notice
